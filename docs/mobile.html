The wallet on your phone

Your phone matches payments locally, keeps its keys in the device keychain, and connects to Bitcoin peers, directly or through your own Tor and I2P gateways, while the app is open and in short background checks iOS allows.

What this means for you

Unexpected payments may appear on the next sync. Fees are estimates, and manual file import restores signing only when the file includes this phone’s recovery words; see what a backup cannot restore. Direct connections expose your IP address to peers; your own Tor or I2P gateways can carry peer connections instead. The read guide, payment guide and recovery guide explain these tradeoffs.

2. What a phone actually is

Constraints that are load-bearing, not stylistic:

  • Outbound TCP only. The app talks to full-node peers over Network.framework. No public inbound listener. Backgrounding stops networking; a background check iOS starts builds it again for one bounded scan. Peers are a small outbound pool (default 3): manual endpoints first, then a persisted good-peers list, then the census list bundled with the app, then DNS-seed results — seeds resolve over DNS-over-HTTPS (Cloudflare dns-json at 1.1.1.1, configurable) and fall back to getaddrinfo if DoH is empty or down; RFC1918/loopback answers are dropped on public networks. Dials race a batch of candidates at a time with a short per-attempt timeout, and report exhaustion instead of spinning forever. Peer routing (Automatic, Direct only or Manual) decides which networks are eligible; DNS seeds are used only when clearnet is, and Tor or I2P candidates only through their gateway. Each release bundles the census list fetched during its build; the app does not download census updates. Saved-peer and DNS discovery continue independently. With DNS as the only source, the diversity policy permits two of the three default slots. No addr gossip, no scoring buckets — a misbehaving peer is dropped and replaced.
  • Foreground, plus short background checks. Winnow syncs while open, and asks iOS for background checks (BGAppRefreshTask and BGProcessingTask, at most every 15 minutes). Each is one scan of up to 2,000 blocks inside the time iOS grants, about 25 seconds for a refresh and up to two minutes for processing, with the foreground's routing, peers and checks; then networking stops. iOS decides when and whether they run; they do not run while the phone is off, after Winnow is force-quit, or with Background App Refresh off. Automatic routing that last reached peers through a gateway skips a check when no gateway answers rather than connect directly with no banner to say so. There is no standing connection and no push: push notifications would require a server that knows your addresses or your txids. A payment you are not looking at is found at the next check or the next open.
  • This-device keychain. Secrets live in the iOS Keychain as kSecAttrAccessibleWhenUnlockedThisDeviceOnly behind a .userPresence access control, with iCloud Keychain sync off. They are not in the app’s ordinary JSON state or device backup. Automatic iCloud backup creates a separate encrypted copy after device authentication unless disabled in Advanced; its wrapping key synchronizes through iCloud Keychain. Recovery words appear only behind an explicit Advanced control gated on device-owner authentication (Back up wallet → Show recovery phrase, fail-closed when no passcode is set), and the Keychain runs the same passcode-or-biometric check itself before it releases the secret, so the gate does not rest on the app’s call sites alone; the check the app passed is handed to that read so you are asked once, not twice. A wallet stored by 0.7.1 or earlier gets the access control on its next open, in place. The recovery-word screen offers an explicit copy action; its pasteboard item is local-only, expires after two minutes, and warns that paper is more private. Signing loads the secret for the call and drops it.
  • Cryptography and transport dependencies. Curve math (key derivation, Taproot tweaks, BIP340 signing) is Bitcoin Core’s libsecp256k1 through the pinned swift-secp256k1 package. Filters, headers, PSBT, descriptors and Bitcoin P2P are in-tree.
  • App Store reality. iOS 17 or later. The app targets iPhone and iPad, with the same iOS app available on Apple silicon Mac. Wallet and protocol behavior lives in the package; app units and one recorded iPhone journey check the interface and its integration.

Bandwidth and battery are real but secondary. Steady-state compact filters are a few megabytes a day (approx.; see read-side §2.5). The worse mobile cost is policy: an always-on mempool subscription, a historical back-scan, or a server that answers getbalance. Those are refused even when the radio would allow them.

3. The four jobs, and two more

What’s mine? BIP157/158 filters, matched on-device, from creation height forward read-side
Where’s the tip? 80-byte headers, PoW + chainwork read-side

Moving a wallet in? A history bundle. There is no back-scan. import

One user-controlled escape hatch crosses several rows: a selected external explorer website. Opening an exact address or transaction requires a warning; its response never becomes wallet state.

4. What the device forces onto the wallet

4.1 Fresh, and forward-only

A new wallet and its recovery phrase are created immediately, offline. Header synchronization and automatic backup continue after onboarding; the wallet starts scanning from the last already-validated height so the interval cannot be missed. Recovering an old wallet privately from the chain would mean gigabytes of historical filters on a radio the user pays for. The product does not do that. Import requires the previous wallet’s answers to come along — descriptor, UTXOs, history, a last-known height — and the phone verifies them by scanning forward from that height. The argument and the bundle format are import.

This is the constraint that makes the rest cheap. It is not a v1 shortcut.

4.2 Taproot only

Receiving is BIP86 P2TR. There is no legacy / nested-segwit / P2WPKH path, and no ECDSA signing path. One output type means one watch-list shape, one sighash, one witness size for fee math (66 bytes for a key-path spend), and no “which address type did this contact use?” branch in the UI.

The phone can pay standard P2PKH, P2SH, P2WPKH, P2WSH and P2TR addresses. Recovery supports descriptor-derived coins; wallet files requiring unsupported signing data are refused. Keep the original wallet and backup for any unsupported coin types. See import and recovery.

4.3 Multisig accounts, same read path

A multisig account is another descriptor and another watch list, scanned with the same filters. The phone runs no coordinator: the PSBT is the coordinator, and any transport that carries text carries it. A paired Ledger is reached over Bluetooth instead: it registers the account, shows addresses and payments on its own screen, and returns a signature the phone verifies before keeping it. Each signer adds an ordinary script-path signature, in any order. A backup from the shared-account Winnow that carries a MuSig2 account is refused rather than restored without those funds.

4.4 Mainnet by default, signet for testing

The app opens on mainnet by default; signet is available in Advanced settings with its own wallet state. The focused UI integration test and its continuous video use a disposable signet fixture. The numbers in screenshots/timings.json are historical signet measurements.

5. Trust on a phone

What the user is asked to trust, stated as mechanisms rather than intentions:

  • The keychain and the device. The default backup file excludes recovery words. An export made with Include recovery phrase contains your phone’s signing secret. Protect that file like the words themselves. Anyone able to unlock your phone may be able to authorize signing or secret access.
  • The peers you can reach. Compact filters are not consensus-committed. Multi-peer cfcheckpt / cfheaders comparison detects disagreement; it cannot prove which peer lied, and a fully eclipsed phone can be shown a consistent lie. Manual peers exist so a user who has a node they trust can skip DNS seeds. The residual risk is read-side §2.7.1 and §2.9.
  • Local filter matching. The wallet does not send its watch list to a history server. Peers still see block requests and connection metadata, and filter matching retains the peer-trust limits described above.

What the user is not asked to trust: an indexer operator’s retention policy or a push-notification vendor. Automatic iCloud backup adds trust in Apple Account and iCloud Keychain security. The app owns device authentication, backup preferences and the encrypted cloud format; WalletCore supplies the Bitcoin rules.

6. Current limits

The app currently omits:

  • Always-on mempool / background 0-conf / live fee histograms
  • Historical filter back-scan
  • BIP37, Electrum, or any “ask a node about my address”
  • An embedded consensus engine or fraud-proof verifier
  • Shared accounts, cosigners or a coordinator server

The focused guides explain the consequences: delayed payment discovery, coarser fees, a required history backup, and transaction disclosure to relay peers.

7. How the implementation is checked

The validation appendix of the read side covers the stack. The focused signet UI integration test drives one receive/send flow through the real binary while the host records a continuous video. The fixture funds the phone and mines confirmation blocks automatically. Detailed behavior and import rules have focused app, wallet, and protocol tests. Guide illustrations and screenshots/timings.json retain their historical provenance; they are not current-run evidence.

CI combines package tests, app unit tests and the recorded iPhone journey. Matching build and test inputs can reuse verified CI evidence; the results and recording retain their original source and run.

8. Status

The app defaults to mainnet and also supports signet. The only signing path is an ordinary Taproot key-path spend with this phone's key. Planned features remain on the roadmap.
